First steps

We started by aligning across product and engineering on what the redesign needed to solve — not just aesthetically, but structurally. The app had grown organically. Features accumulated without a coherent information architecture, and the "More" tab had become a symptom of that drift.

Early work focused on:

  • Analyzing sales data with product to set clear goals and identify key markets
  • Attending quarterly business reviews to understand priorities and get buy-in
  • Facilitating alignment between product and engineering on feature integration
  • Conducting interviews with clients and their customers

Discovery

Discovery

We began by asking two foundational questions:

  • What do consumers expect to see when they first open the app?
  • How do users organize and prioritize their interactions with retail banking features?

Deep-dive interviews helped us understand how people actually used their mobile banking app day-to-day — the habits, shortcuts, and workarounds that the existing design hadn't anticipated.

From there, we designed potential solutions, shared them with PM and engineering before testing, then ran two rounds of concept testing with real bank customers.

Research methods

  • Deep-dive interviews with bank customers
  • Card sorting with customers and internal stakeholders
  • Tree testing of proposed navigation structures
  • 2 rounds of concept testing with iterative design
  • Competitive benchmarking
Concept testing

The key design decision

We tested two structural concepts using the same content to isolate the question of hierarchy, not visuals.

Concept 1

Category-based — organizes around what things are. Mental model: "I want to check my account balance."

Concept 2

Task-based — organizes around what the user wants to do or know. Mental model: "How much can I spend this month?"

The verdict

Neither concept won outright. Users relied on traditional categories when managing specific accounts, but wanted task-oriented answers for spending and goals. The final design uses category-based tabs in the main navigation (Accounts, Cards) while surfacing task-oriented content like spending insights and financial goals on the home dashboard.

Plot twist: Where's the "More" button?

Our goal with the new information architecture was to eliminate vague sections like the "More" tab — which had become an easy-to-implement catch-all dumping ground.

However, our research had a blind spot: several clients still insisted on keeping the "More" tab in the bottom navigation, fearing that users wouldn't be able to find essential functions like settings and logout without it.

So, we developed a hybrid approach: we kept the "More" tab to address client concerns, but established a roadmap to phase it out over time. What mattered was that "More" would no longer be a catch-all for features that didn't fit — it would become a settings and utility drawer, deliberately scoped.

Key product decision

Designing with business buy-in built in

Design decisions that lack cross-functional alignment often get reversed. Attending QBRs and working from sales data meant that the design direction was grounded in business reality from the start.

What changed: Engineering directors and product stakeholders were engaged early and shown research findings directly. Design wasn't a handoff, it was a shared journey throughout the process.

Kept

Durability. Decisions made with cross-functional input were more likely to survive engineering and client review.

Gave up

Speed. Early alignment took time and iteration that a faster, designer-led process would have avoided.
