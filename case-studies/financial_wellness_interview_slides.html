A Financial Wellness experience designed to help users understand not just where their money went, but what to do next.

Sole designer. Led research, concept design, prototyping, and 3 rounds of usability testing. Collaborated closely with Product and Engineering.

First steps

My first step was understanding what insight already existed internally.

We had previously run a financial wellness survey with nearly 300 participants across the US, UK, and Canada, which highlighted:

  • Financial anxiety patterns
  • Decision-making behavior
  • Moments users actively seek guidance
  • Frustrations with existing banking tools

I also revisited analytics from the existing Insights experience and prior research from the broader retail banking redesign. Engagement was low — users briefly checked spending categories or budgets, but deeper interaction was rare.

Layout exploration

Exploring new hierarchy models

Concept 1: Priority-first

Immediate clarity and action. "Available to Spend" as the dominant anchor. One number, one focus.

Concept 2: Topic-first

A balanced guidance model: 'What's my situation right now?' → 'How did I get here?' → 'Where am I headed?' → 'What can I do about it?'

Concept 3: Category-based

Closer to traditional banking taxonomy. Familiar structure, broader financial overview upfront.

Designing around imperfect financial data

A major challenge: the system understands different parts of a user's financial life with very different levels of reliability. The interface can't assume perfect financial understanding.

Reliable
Account balance

Real-time and stable, but can be less meaningful on its own without context around upcoming obligations.

Noisy
Transaction categories

Usually accurate for common spending patterns, but inconsistent with ambiguous merchant names and edge cases.

Uncertain
Forecasting

Works best with recurring patterns and established history. Reliability drops significantly with variable income. Still on roadmap.

Missing
New users

Some users may have little or no historical financial data, making onboarding and progressive personalization critical.

AI-assisted browser prototyping

Once the hierarchy direction became clearer, I moved quickly into AI-assisted browser prototyping.

This enabled earlier discussions around feasibility and design-system alignment, making collaboration with product and engineering more concrete from the start. Interactive browser prototypes proved more effective for discussing behavior and hierarchy than static flows ever were.

Decision 01

Show the calculation, not just the output

The original "available to spend" was a single derived number that ignored upcoming bills.

What changed: A transparent formula (Balance − Upcoming Bills = Left to Spend) that users could edit. The differentiator wasn't the prediction itself, but making the logic visible.

Kept

Trust, accuracy, user agency. The number reflects reality, not an approximation.

Gave up

Simplicity. Showing a formula adds upfront cognitive load.

Editing the amount

Confirmation state

Decision 02

Progressive disclosure

Participant: "It looks like I'm now a data analyst."

What changed: A calmer default focused on what users checked most often. Deeper insights appeared progressively through exploration and expansion.

Kept

Daily usability for users who want quick, clear answers.

Gave up

Discoverability. Some users didn't notice deeper functionality unless they actively explored.

Calm by default, detailed on request

Decision 03

AI as context, not centerpiece

Users were generally comfortable with AI-driven financial guidance, but excessive AI labeling made the interface feel noisy and self-conscious.

What changed: We shifted from highly visible AI interactions toward calmer, contextual guidance. AI became more supportive rather than the centerpiece of the experience.

Kept

Trust and engagement, transparency where it mattered, and contextual guidance tied to real user goals.

Gave up

Highly visible AI branding, "black box" behavior, AI as the primary interaction model.

AI in context with visible, editable logic

Design tensions

Should users need to edit "Left to Spend"?
Concern

Frequent overrides could signal weak forecasting, low confidence, or gaps in the underlying financial data.

Resolution

Users can inspect or occasionally override system assumptions when financial context is uncertain, while the default experience remains calm and mostly automated.

How visible should AI be?
Concern

Too much AI labeling made the interface feel noisy and self-conscious.

Resolution

Use AI where it improves guidance, not where it advertises itself. AI indicators should appear selectively where transparency matters most.

Where we're at now

A recurring question throughout the project was how far the design should go, and how quickly. A full rollout isn't realistic in one step. So I designed two tracks in parallel: the long-term vision, and a deliberate first step MVP.

The MVP and hybrid designs continue to evolve, shaping internal conversations about the Financial Wellness roadmap and how AI guidance can be introduced progressively across markets.

Looking ahead

What I'm doing next

A few areas worth digging into as this work continues.

Deeper personalization strategy

Understanding whether personas should shape hierarchy, content priority, or both — and how that changes with data richness over time.

Payday-aware forecasting

The data infrastructure is close. The design question is how to introduce it without raising anxiety around uncertainty.

Longitudinal engagement

Does the Hybrid design hold up over time, or does novelty fade? That's still an open question.

Terminology validation

"Left to Spend" tested well in the UK. Needs validation across other markets before it can scale.

Dashboard customization

There's appetite for it. The question is whether the cognitive cost of configuration is worth the personalization payoff.
