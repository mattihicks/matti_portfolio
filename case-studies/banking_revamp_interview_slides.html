First steps

We started by aligning across product and engineering on what the redesign needed to solve, not just aesthetically, but structurally. The app had grown organically, and the "More" tab had become a symptom of that drift.

  • Analyzed sales data with product to set clear goals and identify key markets
  • Attended quarterly business reviews to understand priorities and get buy-in
  • Conducted interviews with clients and their customers
  • Expert review: 61 expert review findings, including untappable buttons and small text

Exploratory research

We began by asking two foundational questions:

  • What do consumers expect to see when they first open the app?
  • How do users organize and prioritize their interactions with retail banking features?

Deep-dive interviews helped us understand how people actually used their mobile banking day-to-day. From there, we shared concepts with PM and engineering, then ran two rounds of concept testing with real bank customers.

Concept testing

Concept 1

Category-based — organizes around what things are. Mental model: "I want to check my account balance."

Concept 2

Task-based — organizes around what the user wants to do or know. Mental model: "How much can I spend this month?"

Plot twist: Where's the "More" button?

Our goal was to eliminate the "More" tab — an easy-to-implement catch-all dumping ground. But several clients still insisted on keeping it, fearing users wouldn't find settings and logout without it.

We developed a hybrid: keep "More" scoped deliberately, with a roadmap to phase it out over time.

Key product decision

Designing with business buy-in built in

Design decisions that lack cross-functional alignment often get reversed. Attending QBRs and working from sales data meant the design direction was grounded in business reality from the start.

What changed: Engineering directors and product stakeholders were engaged early and shown research findings directly. Design wasn't a handoff, it was a shared journey throughout the process.

Kept

Durability. Decisions made with cross-functional input were more likely to survive engineering and client review.

Gave up

Speed. Early alignment took time and iteration that a faster, designer-led process would have avoided.
